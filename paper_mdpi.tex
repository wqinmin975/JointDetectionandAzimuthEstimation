\abstract{The proliferation of UAVs in aerial photography, logistics, agriculture, and surveillance has created a demand for counter-UAV sensing that is passive, low-cost, and independent of illumination. Microphone arrays built from micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) sensors meet these requirements, but most acoustic systems treat drone detection and direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation as decoupled stages, preventing feature sharing and letting detection errors propagate into localization. This paper studies end-to-end networks for passive drone detection and $180^\circ$ azimuth-axis estimation from the raw multichannel waveforms of a six-element MEMS uniform linear array (ULA). Its engineering contributions are a geometry-aware generalized cross-correlation with phase transform (GCC-PHAT) frontend with true-array-geometry adaptation for recorded audio and a Neyman--Pearson (NP) calibration of the detection threshold. A shared 1D-CNN backbone feeds a detection head and a regression head; uncertainty-based dynamic weighting removes the need for manual loss-weight search. Monte Carlo simulations compare the learned model with classical TDOA baselines GCC-PHAT and steered-response power with phase transform (SRP-PHAT): at $\mathrm{SNR}=-5\,\mathrm{dB}$ it attains $P_d=1.00$, $P_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.00$, MAE $7.50^\circ\pm10.38^\circ$, against $46.40^\circ\pm26.81^\circ$ and $46.16^\circ\pm25.04^\circ$ for GCC-PHAT and SRP-PHAT. The classical baselines stall near the folded-chance level because the narrowband tonal carrier makes PHAT's delay estimate period-ambiguous---its cross-correlation has several nearly equivalent peaks---so their error is set by multi-peak ambiguity rather than by noise level. Properly trained single-task localization and fixed equal weighting reach $5.95^\circ\pm10.84^\circ$ (Solo-Loc) and $5.62^\circ\pm10.30^\circ$ (fixed $\lambda=1$), at least as accurate as the joint model. The accuracy gain therefore comes from the learned end-to-end representation, not from multi-task joint training (no measurable gain) or from dynamic weighting (whose only benefit is the absence of manual loss-weight tuning). Detection is saturated at every tested SNR, so the simulation does not exercise the detection operating regime. Recorded-drone validation uses a \emph{separately trained} eight-microphone network (JointDetDOANet): a $28\times61$ GCC-PHAT frontend feeding its own Conv1d($28,32$) backbone, pretrained on the corrected simulation and then fine-tuned on the Fraunhofer IDMT corpus~\cite{idmt2025drone}; the six-microphone raw-waveform network of the simulation is not reused and no weights are shared. Fine-tuning reduces the $180^\circ$-folded azimuth MAE from $45.55^\circ$ to $18.05^\circ$---a folded error on the $180^\circ$ azimuth axis, not a full $360^\circ$ bearing, and an optimistic in-sample estimate rather than a generalization result. In a separate v3-architecture re-run with unfurled $360^\circ$ labels, full-circle MAE falls from $138.4^\circ$ to $31.9^\circ$, at the cost of a higher folded error ($24.4^\circ$ versus $19.1^\circ$ in that re-run); full-circle capability is thus reported as a separate result and not inferred from the folded metric.}

\keyword{direction-of-arrival estimation; MEMS microphone array; multi-task learning; UAV detection; Cram\'er--Rao bound}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are now widely deployed for photography, delivery, agriculture, and infrastructure monitoring. Flights that violate airspace regulations, however, pose risks to public safety and privacy, and this has motivated sustained research on UAV detection and localization~\cite{taha2019drone}. Radio-frequency analysis, radar, and electro-optical sensors address this problem but are either active emitters, dependent on illumination, or too costly for wide-area deployment. Acoustic sensing has its own pain points: wind and traffic noise mask rotor harmonics, reverberation smears inter-channel delays, and competing sources confound a single-source assumption. Passive acoustic sensing with arrays of low-cost MEMS microphones is nevertheless an attractive complement: it requires no emission, operates at night and in poor visibility, and has been demonstrated for UAV detection and tracking in several field studies~\cite{case2008uav,busset2015acoustic}. Practical MEMS arrays are, however, imperfect devices: channel gain (sensitivity) mismatch, intermittent channel failure, geometry calibration error, and element self-noise all perturb the assumed array manifold~\cite{yan2023mems}. These four effects are the most important gap between idealized simulation and real MEMS hardware, and they motivate the geometry-aware frontend of Section~\ref{sec:geo} as a first step toward tolerance of non-ideal arrays; the simulation itself still assumes an ideal manifold, and explicit MEMS-impairment modeling is left to future work.

A spinning rotor radiates a broadband, amplitude-modulated acoustic signature, and the inter-channel time delay of this signature across an array constrains the source direction. Classical pipelines estimate the direction of arrival (DOA) with generalized cross-correlation (GCC-PHAT)~\cite{knapp1976gcc}, steered-response power (SRP-PHAT)~\cite{dibiase2001srp}, or subspace methods such as MUSIC~\cite{schmidt1986music} and ESPRIT~\cite{roy1989esprit}, while a separate energy- or spectrum-based test decides whether a drone is present at all. This decoupling has two drawbacks: detection errors propagate directly into the localization stage, and the two stages cannot share the features they extract from the same signal. Their performance also degrades markedly at low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

Learned estimators improve robustness by mapping array observations directly to angles. DOA-Net~\cite{adavanne2018doa} estimates the DOA of multiple sources from phase spectra with a convolutional recurrent network; SS-CNN~\cite{nguyen2020sscnn} combines a spatial pseudo-spectrum with a convolutional network for joint source counting and DOA estimation; and attention- or transformer-based estimators~\cite{vaswani2017attention,wang2024transformer,mack2022attention} further improve accuracy at low SNR. In parallel, deep networks have been applied to drone sound detection in realistic environments~\cite{jeon2017drone,kim2017uav}. Existing learning-based DOA estimators, however, assume the target source is present and do not output a detection decision, whereas drone-detection networks cannot estimate the source azimuth. A small number of acoustic multi-task DOA studies do exist, but they typically assume a fixed number of sources, predict class probabilities over discrete directional bins rather than a continuous bearing, or omit a properly trained single-task control; consequently, a simultaneous existence probability plus continuous azimuth regression output is rare, and the assumed benefit of joint training is seldom tested against such a control. We therefore do not take the common claim that joint training is necessarily beneficial as given: we test it directly with matched single-task references and fixed-weight ablations, rather than inferring it from a comparison with an untrained head or a classical baseline.

We address this gap with an end-to-end network that jointly detects a drone and regresses its azimuth from raw multichannel waveforms, and we ask explicitly whether the joint design and the loss-weighting rule deliver an accuracy gain. Because the two tasks share one backbone, their losses must be balanced during training; we adopt uncertainty-based dynamic weighting~\cite{kendall2018mtl}, which removes manual loss-weight tuning, and compare it against fixed-weight baselines and properly trained single-task references. As detailed below, the joint design and the dynamic rule do not improve localization accuracy in our simulation, so we present them as design choices with a calibration benefit rather than as sources of accuracy. We separate \emph{performance} claims from \emph{engineering-tool} claims and group the contributions below into two independent categories: (i) algorithmic/performance contributions, which change the achievable azimuth error, and (ii) engineering-tool contributions, which change how the system is calibrated or deployed without lowering the error themselves. Dynamic weighting buys freedom from tuning, not accuracy, and the NP threshold is a calibration strategy, not an MAE reducer.

\paragraph{(i) Algorithmic and performance contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Network architecture.} An end-to-end multi-task architecture that performs drone detection and azimuth regression from the raw multichannel waveform of a six-element MEMS ULA through a shared one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) backbone, assessed against properly trained single-task references rather than assumed beneficial;
    \item \emph{Controlled simulation comparison.} A Monte Carlo evaluation showing that the learned model substantially outperforms classical GCC-PHAT and SRP-PHAT, while properly trained single-task localization and detection references match the joint model, so no accuracy gain is claimed for joint training; the accuracy gain is attributed to the learned end-to-end representation;
    \item \emph{Recorded-data validation.} Fine-tuning on the Fraunhofer IDMT corpus lowers the $180^\circ$-folded azimuth MAE from $45.55^\circ$ (zero-shot) to $18.05^\circ$---an optimistic in-sample estimate on a folded $180^\circ$ axis, not a full-circle generalization result---while a v3-architecture re-run with unfurled labels separately reduces full-circle error from $138.4^\circ$ to $31.9^\circ$, and a WSU evaluation shows that classical detectors degrade to chance under loud ambient noise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(ii) Engineering-tool contributions (no accuracy gain claimed).}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Loss design and gradient analysis.} An uncertainty-based dynamic loss-weighting scheme that removes manual loss-weight search, together with a closed-form effective-weight ($\lambda_t$) analysis of how the two task gradients are traded. In our experiments it is not more accurate than fixed equal weighting, so its value is the absence of a tuning sweep rather than higher peak precision;
    \item \emph{Neyman--Pearson threshold calibration.} An NP operating-point selection for the detection head that delivers an SNR-robust threshold $\gamma^*\approx0.086$ for the prescribed $1\%$ false-alarm target, while the minimum-error default $\gamma=0.5$ is retained only as a reporting operating point: its false-alarm count is zero, but 0/180 negatives cannot establish $P_{\mathrm{fa}}<1\%$. This is a calibration strategy and does not by itself reduce azimuth MAE. In a counter-UAV application the default $\gamma=0.5$ is in fact unsuitable as an operating point: a minimum-error rule does not control the false-alarm probability, and 0/180 negatives only place an upper bound (95\% confidence $\approx1.7\%$) on $P_{\mathrm{fa}}$, so it cannot certify the required $1\%$ target. A Neyman--Pearson calibration is therefore needed to set a threshold with a guaranteed false-alarm rate;
    \item \emph{Cross-array preprocessing frontend.} A geometry-aware GCC-PHAT frontend and the use of the true cubic-array geometry in a \emph{separately trained} eight-microphone network (28-channel GCC-PHAT input, its own Conv1d($28,32$) backbone), pretrained on the corrected simulation and then applied to recorded drone audio; the six-microphone raw-waveform network is not reused and no weights are shared, so no zero-shot transfer of six-channel weights to 28-channel inputs is attempted.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Classical DOA Estimation}
TDOA-based localization has been studied extensively. GCC-PHAT~\cite{knapp1976gcc} and SRP-PHAT~\cite{dibiase2001srp} remain standard baselines because of their simplicity, and subspace-based methods such as MUSIC~\cite{schmidt1986music} and ESPRIT~\cite{roy1989esprit} achieve higher angular resolution under favorable conditions. The accuracy of all of these methods degrades in noise and reverberation, and the variance---hence the RMSE---of any unbiased estimator is bounded from below by the Cram\'er--Rao bound (CRB)~\cite{stoica1989music}. A mechanism specific to our simulation is worth anticipating here: the rotor signal of~\eqref{eq:rotor} is dominated by an \SI{800}{\hertz} carrier, whose period is $20$ sampling intervals at $f_s=\SI{16}{\kilo\hertz}$, whereas the largest inter-element delay of the $d=\SI{0.08}{\meter}$ ULA is only $df_s/c\approx3.73$ samples. The TDOA is therefore defined only modulo a full carrier period, so the cross-correlation has several nearly equivalent peaks and PHAT's magnitude whitening gives the noise-dominated bins the same weight as the few tonal bins; the delay estimate becomes effectively periodic-ambiguous rather than noise-limited. This empirical mechanism explains why both GCC-PHAT and SRP-PHAT sit near the folded-chance level in Section~\ref{sec:results} and why their error barely responds to SNR.

\subsection{Learned DOA Estimation and Drone Acoustics}
Machine learning has been applied broadly to acoustic problems~\cite{bianco2019ml}. For DOA estimation, DOA-Net~\cite{adavanne2018doa} maps phase spectra to per-source angles, SS-CNN~\cite{nguyen2020sscnn} learns from a spatial pseudo-spectrum, and attention-based architectures~\cite{vaswani2017attention} have been adapted to array processing, including DOA estimation for uniform linear arrays at low SNR~\cite{wang2024transformer} and for general acoustic signals~\cite{mack2022attention}. For UAV monitoring specifically, acoustic detection with deep networks has been validated on field recordings~\cite{jeon2017drone,kim2017uav}, and acoustic camera arrays have been used for drone tracking~\cite{busset2015acoustic}; a survey of machine-learning-based drone detection is given in~\cite{taha2019drone}. Almost all learned DOA estimators presume that the source is present and output no existence probability, while deep-learning studies of UAV acoustics mostly stop at binary detection and never output an angle. These works therefore address either detection or localization in isolation.

\subsection{Multi-Task Learning}
Multi-task learning shares a common representation across related tasks; however, fixed linear combinations of per-task losses are sensitive to relative loss-magnitude scales. Kendall \emph{et al.}~\cite{kendall2018mtl} weight each loss by a learned task uncertainty, and GradNorm~\cite{chen2018gradnorm} instead normalizes gradient magnitudes across tasks; GradNorm has also been applied to acoustic multi-task settings. We adopt the uncertainty-based formulation because it yields a closed-form, directly interpretable effective weight $\lambda_t$ in the two-task setting and introduces no extra hyperparameters (Section~\ref{sec:method}). GradNorm instead reweights by the tasks' gradient norms and needs an additional loss term whose normalization constants must be tuned per task, providing no closed-form $\lambda_t$; it would therefore obscure the explicit per-task gradient analysis used to interpret the results. Our experiment finds the dynamic rule no more accurate than fixed equal weighting, a result we report as a cautionary reference for related acoustic multi-task studies that adopt dynamic weighting by default.

\subsection{UAV Acoustic Multi-Task Learning}
The combination of drone detection and DOA regression in one acoustic network is a recent and small literature. Reported UAV acoustic multi-task systems often evaluate the joint model against classical baselines or against an untrained/partial head rather than against separately trained single-task networks with matched capacity and training budget. Such controls matter: without them, a gain attributable to the shared representation can be mistakenly credited to the joint training objective itself. Our study is designed to close this gap by reporting properly trained Solo-Det and Solo-Loc references and fixed-weight ablations alongside the dynamic-weighting model. We stress that this counter-intuitive observational finding on joint training is specific to the simulated single-source, ideal-array setting and should be read as an invitation to test the same hypothesis under other acoustic conditions, not as a general refutation of multi-task learning. For the same reason we do not re-implement DOA-Net, SS-CNN, or transformer-based DOA estimators here: they consume phase spectra or spatial pseudo-spectra produced by a fixed frontend, whereas our network regresses azimuth directly from the raw multichannel waveform, and their parameter and compute budgets differ from ours. A head-to-head comparison would therefore be unfair rather than informative, as stated in Section~\ref{sec:exp}; we defer it to future work.

\section{Signal Model and Simulation}
\label{sec:signal}
We consider a ULA of $M=6$ MEMS microphones with element spacing $d=\SI{0.08}{\meter}$ and sound speed $c=343\ \mathrm{m\cdot s^{-1}}$. A far-field drone at azimuth $\theta$ emits a rotor signal $s(t)$; the $m$-th microphone, $m=0,\dots,M-1$, receives
\begin{equation}
    x_m(t) = s\bigl(t - \tau_m(\theta)\bigr) + n_m(t), \quad \tau_m(\theta)=\frac{m d \cos\theta}{c},
    \label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}
where $n_m(t)$ is additive white Gaussian noise scaled to a prescribed SNR.\footnote{An early development version computed the inter-element delays but did not apply them to the individual channels, leaving the synthetic data without azimuth-dependent cues. That defect has been corrected; all experiments in this paper use the corrected delay model (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).} The synthetic rotor signature consists of an \SI{800}{\hertz} carrier amplitude-modulated by a \SI{120}{\hertz} rotor tone,
\begin{equation}
    s(t)=\sin(2\pi\cdot 800\,t)\cdot\bigl[0.6\,(1+0.4\sin(2\pi\cdot 120\,t))\bigr].
    \label{eq:rotor}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry} depicts the two test-bed geometries and the azimuth convention used throughout.

The dataset contains positive samples (drone present, with $\theta$ drawn uniformly from $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$) and negative noise-only samples, with raw multichannel waveforms preserved as network input. Training, validation, and test draws are disjoint: the held-out validation set is used only for threshold selection, every test metric is computed on freshly generated trials, and the SNR of a training sample is drawn at random from the prescribed SNR grid while each test SNR is evaluated independently without retraining (details in Section~\ref{sec:exp}). Because the source is a simplified amplitude-modulated single tone rather than a multi-rotor harmonic spectrum, this simulation necessarily creates a simulation-to-reality domain shift; the large zero-shot error on recorded audio in Section~\ref{sec:real} is consistent with that simplification. The Monte Carlo study of Sections~\ref{sec:exp}--\ref{sec:results} relies on this synthetic data only; the recorded-audio validation of Section~\ref{sec:real} additionally uses the Fraunhofer IDMT corpus described below.

For the single-source conditional model of~\eqref{eq:signal}, the CRB for the azimuth of a far-field source observed by a ULA under additive white Gaussian noise follows the standard form~\cite{stoica1989music} under the narrowband approximation
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CRB}(\theta)=\frac{c^{2}\,\sigma_n^{2}}{2 N_s\,\sigma_s^{2}\,(2\pi f_c)^{2}\,d^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\textstyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}(m-\bar m)^{2}},
    \label{eq:crb}
\end{equation}
Expression~\eqref{eq:crb} is a narrowband, single-carrier result. It bounds the variance---and hence the RMSE---of an unbiased estimator, \emph{not} the MAE reported below, and it is evaluated at broadside ($\theta=90^\circ$) only; it is used as an RMSE-type reference rather than as a lower bound on the tabulated MAE (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
where $\sigma_s^{2}$ and $\sigma_n^{2}$ are the signal and noise powers, $N_s$ is the number of temporal samples in the observation window (2048, matched to the network input), $f_c$ is the carrier frequency, and $\bar m=\frac{M-1}{2}$. The simulated rotor signature of~\eqref{eq:rotor} has modulation index $0.4$, so most of its power remains in the carrier at $f_c=\SI{800}{\hertz}$ and the two sidebands at $f_c\pm\SI{120}{\hertz}$ carry comparatively little power; this is why the narrowband approximation is informative at all.

\section{Proposed Multi-Task Joint Network}
\label{sec:method}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the architecture of the six-microphone raw-waveform network used for the simulation (JointDetDOANetRaw). Its shared 1D-CNN backbone maps the $6\times2048$ input through a Conv1d($6,32$) first layer, and its flattened latent vector feeds both the detection head and the DOA regression head; the 3-D microphone coordinates are encoded by a small MLP, mean-pooled, and concatenated with the backbone features before the heads. The recorded-audio experiments of Section~\ref{sec:real} use a \emph{different, independently trained} network (JointDetDOANet) with a geometry-aware GCC-PHAT frontend; its weights are not derived from, or shared with, the network shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}.

\subsection{Shared 1D-CNN Backbone}
The raw $M\times N$ waveform is processed by a shared 1D-CNN that stacks temporal convolutions and pooling layers and flattens the result into a fixed-length latent vector used by both heads.

\subsection{Detection and DOA Heads}
Two lightweight heads branch from the latent vector:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a \emph{detection head} that outputs a scalar probability $p\in[0,1]$ (via sigmoid activation) for drone presence, trained using binary cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$;
    \item a \emph{DOA head} that regresses the continuous azimuth $\hat\theta$, trained with an $\ell_1$ loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dynamic Multi-Task Loss}
A common way to combine the two objectives is the fixed-weight form
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fix}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}},
    \label{eq:fixed}
\end{equation}
but a fixed $\lambda$ must be chosen by hand and trades detection against localization (Section~\ref{sec:results}). We therefore adopt the uncertainty-based balancing of Kendall \emph{et al.}~\cite{kendall2018mtl}, which learns this trade-off automatically, introducing one learnable scalar uncertainty $\sigma_k$ per task and minimizing
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{cls}}^{2}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}} + \frac{1}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{reg}}^{2}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}} + \log\!\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{cls}}\sigma_{\mathrm{reg}}\right).
    \label{eq:dynamic}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dynamic} lends itself to a physical reading: $\sigma_{\mathrm{cls}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{reg}}$ act as learnable ``observation noise'' levels for the classification and regression tasks, respectively---a task-wise homoscedastic uncertainty term---so that the precision factors $1/(2\sigma_k^{2})$ measure how reliable each task's residuals currently are. The gradient of~\eqref{eq:dynamic} with respect to the shared parameters $\mathbf{w}$ is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{cls}}^{2}}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}} + \frac{1}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{reg}}^{2}}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}},
    \label{eq:grad}
\end{equation}
so the effective convex weight on the classification gradient at training step $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_t = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{reg}}^{2}(t)}{\sigma_{\mathrm{cls}}^{2}(t)+\sigma_{\mathrm{reg}}^{2}(t)}.
    \label{eq:lambda_t}
\end{equation}
Physically, $\lambda_t$ is the effective \emph{share} of the classification gradient in the combined parameter update: the complementary share $1-\lambda_t=\sigma_{\mathrm{cls}}^{2}(t)/(\sigma_{\mathrm{cls}}^{2}(t)+\sigma_{\mathrm{reg}}^{2}(t))$ is carried by the regression gradient. For example, $\lambda_t=0.9$ means that $90\%$ of the total precision-weighted gradient coefficient comes from the detection task and only $10\%$ from localization; a value near $0.5$ corresponds to effectively equal weighting. Monitoring $\lambda_t$ over training therefore gives a direct read-out of how the learned uncertainties trade the two tasks (a gradient-share interpretation, not a claim about task accuracy). In practice we optimize the log-variances $s_k=\log\sigma_k^{2}$ jointly with the network weights; this keeps $\sigma_k^{2}$ positive and the regularization term $\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{cls}}\sigma_{\mathrm{reg}})$ in~\eqref{eq:dynamic} prevents the trivial solution $\sigma_k^{2}\!\to\!\infty$. One caveat: the precision factor $1/(2\sigma_k^{2})$ can grow without bound if a task uncertainty collapses toward zero, risking instability; under our setup (log-variance parameterization, Adam $\eta=10^{-3}$, 20 epochs) training stayed stable with no clipping, but we recommend gradient clipping for noisier data or longer budgets. The ablation study in Section~\ref{sec:results} compares~\eqref{eq:dynamic} with the fixed-weight variants of~\eqref{eq:fixed} for $\lambda\in\{0.1,1.0,2.0\}$.

\subsection{Geometry-Aware Frontend for Array Transfer}
\label{sec:geo}
The raw-waveform backbone of Section~\ref{sec:method}-A is tied to the fixed six-element ULA geometry and its first convolutional layer takes six input channels (Conv1d($6,32$); network JointDetDOANetRaw). To handle arrays with a different element count and layout, the real-data experiments of Section~\ref{sec:real} employ a \emph{separately trained} network (JointDetDOANet), not the same weights presented with a different input: it replaces the raw input with a \emph{geometry-aware GCC-PHAT frontend}, so its first convolutional layer takes 28 input channels (Conv1d($28,32$)): for every microphone pair, the phase-transform cross-correlation~\cite{knapp1976gcc} is computed frame-wise and averaged, and a window of $61$ lags around zero delay is retained, yielding a $28\times61$ feature map for an eight-element array in which the azimuth information is concentrated in the correlation lag. In addition, the 3-D microphone coordinates are encoded by a two-layer perceptron; the resulting features are mean-pooled and concatenated with backbone features before being fed into the task heads, so that the heads are explicitly conditioned on the array geometry. A controlled ablation (same random seed, training data, and initialization; $n=600$ test trials at $\mathrm{SNR}=-5\,\mathrm{dB}$) shows that removing the geometry encoder increases the MAE from $2.25^\circ\pm1.79^\circ$ to $2.95^\circ\pm2.30^\circ$: the paired error difference is $+0.70^\circ$ (95\% CI $[0.526^\circ,0.870^\circ]$; paired $t=7.96$, $p<10^{-14}$; 5000-label permutation $p<2\times10^{-4}$), i.e.\ geometry encoding lowers the error by about $31\%$. This confirms that the module contributes to accuracy, rather than merely adding parameters. The caveat is scope: the six-element ULA raw-waveform network has no geometry encoder, so this ablation applies only to the 28-channel GCC-PHAT network used for recorded-audio validation. The detection and DOA heads and the form of the dynamic loss~\eqref{eq:dynamic} are unchanged, but the two networks are trained independently and share no parameters. The real-data results below are obtained by pretraining this 28-channel network on the corrected simulation and then fine-tuning it on the recorded corpus; there is no step in which six-channel weights are fed zero-shot into the 28-channel network.
\paragraph{Computational cost.}
The six-channel raw-waveform network has 36,078 parameters (22.9 MFLOPs per forward pass), whereas the separately trained 28-channel GCC-PHAT network is smaller (24,618 parameters; 0.629 MFLOPs per forward pass). We note that the latter figure covers the learned network only: the GCC-PHAT frontend is a fixed, non-parametric FFT-based preprocessing stage whose cost is not included.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp}
The simulation uses sampling rate $f_s=\SI{16}{\kilo\hertz}$, $M=6$ ULA elements, and an SNR sweep of $\{-10,-5,0,5,10,15\}\,\mathrm{dB}$. The training set comprises 4000 samples: $70\%$ drone-positive samples with the azimuth drawn uniformly from $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$ and $30\%$ pure-noise negative samples. A separate held-out validation set of 500 samples, generated by the same process with the same $30\%$ negative ratio and excluded from training, is reserved for detection-threshold selection, whereas all reported test metrics are computed on freshly generated Monte Carlo samples (600 per SNR) that are statistically independent of the training data. Negative samples are white Gaussian noise, and the channel noise added to positive samples is white Gaussian noise scaled to the prescribed SNR. Each training sample is assigned an SNR drawn uniformly at random from the six-level set $\{-10,-5,0,5,10,15\}\,\mathrm{dB}$ (equal probability), so a single model is exposed to the full range and is then evaluated across all SNRs without retraining. The network is trained with Adam ($\eta=10^{-3}$) for $20$ epochs with batch size $32$; the same trained model is evaluated at all SNR levels via Monte Carlo trials. The proposed network and the classical GCC-PHAT/SRP-PHAT baselines share the same synthetic signal model, array geometry, delay model and estimator code. The network entries of Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare} are taken from the per-SNR Monte Carlo protocol above (600 freshly drawn trials per SNR, statistically independent of the training data). The classical entries of the same table come from an \emph{independent} Monte Carlo draw that does not reuse the network test samples: it uses 60 trials whose SNR is drawn uniformly from the same six-level set instead of being pinned to $-5\,\mathrm{dB}$, and its detection operating point is taken from a separate 600-trial mixed-SNR energy-detector draw. The two families are therefore not paired on identical noise realizations, and no paired statistical test is claimed. The comparison nevertheless remains meaningful because the classical azimuth error is essentially independent of SNR (Fig.~\ref{fig:snr}), so a mixed-SNR mean lies within a fraction of a degree of any single-SNR value; the quoted $\pm$std and Monte Carlo standard errors are of comparable magnitude but derive from different draws. Baselines include the classical TDOA estimators GCC-PHAT~\cite{knapp1976gcc} and SRP-PHAT~\cite{dibiase2001srp}; learned DOA estimators (DOA-Net~\cite{adavanne2018doa}, SS-CNN~\cite{nguyen2020sscnn}, transformer~\cite{wang2024transformer,mack2022attention}) are discussed in related work but are not re-evaluated here. GCC-PHAT and SRP-PHAT are localization-only and have no intrinsic detector; for a fair comparison we equip them with a broadband energy test whose statistic is the total array energy $\sum_m\|x_m\|^2$ and whose threshold is the $95$th percentile of the noise-only energy, yielding an operating point near $P_{\mathrm{fa}}\approx0.05$. The classical baselines' $P_d/P_{\mathrm{fa}}$ quoted in Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare} are obtained from that same independent energy-detector draw (the broadband energy threshold is calibrated to the $95$th percentile of the noise-only energy), not from the $-5\,\mathrm{dB}$ anchor at which the network rows are reported; on a uniform linear array SRP-PHAT reduces to a GCC-PHAT-like delay-sum, which is why the two baselines give nearly identical MAE. We therefore focus our benchmark comparison on classical TDOA-based approaches and defer a head-to-head comparison with deep-learning DOA networks to future work; the reasons are that networks such as DOA-Net, SS-CNN, and transformer-based estimators consume phase spectra or spatial pseudo-spectra produced by a fixed frontend, whereas the present work regresses the azimuth directly from the raw multichannel waveform, so the input representation, model capacity, and computational budget differ across the two families and a head-to-head comparison would be unfair. All simulation results in this paper are fully reproducible from the accompanying code.\footnote{The main script \texttt{drone\_acoustic\_joint.py} regenerates all tables and figures in one run (\texttt{--mode sim}); it exports \texttt{data\_snr.csv} for Figure~\ref{fig:snr} and \texttt{table1\_repro.csv} for Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare}. The classical curves overlaid on Figure~\ref{fig:snr} are produced by \texttt{baseline\_snr.py}, which exports \texttt{baseline\_snr.csv}; it draws all randomness from locally seeded generators, so repeated runs give bit-identical output. Every script fixes its random seed before generating data or training, so the reported tables and curves are reproduced exactly. The code is released in a public repository at \url{https://github.com/wqinmin975/JointDetectionandAzimuthEstimation} and is also available from the corresponding author upon request during the review period.}

\subsection{Detection Threshold and Operating Point}
\label{sec:np}
Let $H_0$ denote the noise-only hypothesis and $H_1$ the drone-present hypothesis. The detection head outputs $p=\mathbb{P}(H_1\mid\boldsymbol{x})$, and a detection is declared when $p>\gamma$. The operating metrics are
\begin{equation}
    P_d(\gamma)=\mathbb{P}(p>\gamma\mid H_1), \qquad P_{\mathrm{fa}}(\gamma)=\mathbb{P}(p>\gamma\mid H_0),
    \label{eq:pdpfa}
\end{equation}
estimated over the Monte Carlo trials. On the held-out validation set of 500 samples we select, for each target false-alarm rate, the smallest threshold $\gamma^*$ that satisfies the Neyman--Pearson constraint $P_{\mathrm{fa}}(\gamma)\le P_{\mathrm{fa}}^{\mathrm{target}}$; sweeping $\gamma$ over $[0,1]$ yields the operating points reported below; because the detector is already near-saturated ($P_d\approx0.99$) at the minimum-error threshold in this synthetic regime, the resulting characteristic is near-degenerate, so we summarize it by the selected $\gamma^*$ operating points rather than plotting the full curve. For the target $P_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.01$, the selected threshold is $\gamma^*=0.086$ at every tested SNR from $-10$ to $15\,\mathrm{dB}$. It gives a validation false-alarm rate of $0.0066$ and, at $\gamma^*$, a test-set detection probability of $1.00$ with a realized test false-alarm rate of $0.005$. Relative to the default $\gamma=0.5$, the calibrated threshold also lifts the $-5\,\mathrm{dB}$ test detection probability from $0.99$ to $1.00$. These point estimates carry a Monte Carlo standard error of about $0.4\%$ (roughly 420 positive test samples at this SNR), so the two operating points are consistent within noise. Relaxing the target to $P_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.05$ lowers the threshold to $\gamma^*=0.038$ (validation $P_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.046$) and yields the same test detection probability of $1.00$ at a realized test false-alarm rate of $0.016$. The constancy of $\gamma^*$ across SNR reflects the large separation between the two hypothesis score distributions in this simulation: the constraint-optimal threshold is flat in SNR rather than rising as the SNR decreases, so a single threshold is adequate across the full tested range here. This convenience is a property of the synthetic data and should not be expected to transfer: under a harder regime, such as real non-stationary background noise, the Neyman--Pearson selection framework would need to be recalibrated, a direction flagged in the future work. The WSU experiment of Section~\ref{sec:real_wsu} confirms this warning empirically: classical energy and SRP-power detectors collapse toward chance in loud ambient noise, so a constant $\gamma^*$ must not be assumed portable across environments. Unless stated otherwise, all reported results use the default $\gamma=0.5$, the minimum-error decision under balanced priors. This default is \emph{not} the threshold that implements the $1\%$ false-alarm requirement: that requirement is met by the NP-selected $\gamma^*=0.086$. At $\gamma=0.5$ the observed false-alarm count is zero on the $150$ negative validation samples (and on the $180$ negative test samples at the $-5\,\mathrm{dB}$ anchor), but zero observed alarms in samples of this size only places an upper 95\% confidence bound of about $1.7\%$ on $P_{\mathrm{fa}}$; it does not demonstrate $P_{\mathrm{fa}}<1\%$. We therefore use $\gamma=0.5$ as a minimum-error reporting operating point and $\gamma^*$ whenever a formal false-alarm constraint is required.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
We report the detection probability $P_d$, the false-alarm rate $P_{\mathrm{fa}}$, and the DOA mean absolute error (MAE), with the CRB of~\eqref{eq:crb} used only as a broadside RMSE-type reference for unbiased estimators under the narrowband approximation, not as a lower bound on MAE (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
All azimuth errors in this paper are defined on a $180^\circ$ \emph{azimuth axis}, not on the full $0^\circ$--$360^\circ$ compass: training labels and predictions are folded to $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$ and the error is the folded circular error $\min(|\hat\theta-\theta|,180^\circ-|\hat\theta-\theta|)$. This folded metric is a deliberate evaluation convention that matches the front--back ambiguity of the arrays studied here (Sections~\ref{sec:real_idmt} and~\ref{sec:real_wsu}); it is \emph{not} full-circle bearing estimation. A separate $360^\circ$ retraining experiment (Section~\ref{sec:real_idmt}) reports full-circle results explicitly; the folded and full-circle metrics are not interchangeable. Its chance level is $45^\circ$, the expectation of the folded difference of two independent uniform azimuths, whereas the chance level of the unfolded $360^\circ$ circular error is $90^\circ$. An error near $45^\circ$ therefore denotes chance performance under this convention, and the real-data errors reported below must be read with this folding in mind.

\subsection{Code Verification and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
The accompanying code reproduces every table and figure in one run (\texttt{--mode sim}); it exports \texttt{data\_snr.csv} (Fig.~\ref{fig:snr}) and \texttt{table1\_repro.csv} (Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare}). During development we identified and corrected a defect in the simulation's delay model: inter-microphone delays were computed but never applied to the individual channels, leaving the synthetic data without azimuth-dependent cues. The correction is used for every experiment reported here, the released repository contains the fix, and no defective-model results are reported. As a sanity check, after the correction the trained network's azimuth MAE ($7.50^\circ$ at $\mathrm{SNR}=-5\,\mathrm{dB}$) is far below both the $90^\circ$ uniform-random baseline and the $\approx46^\circ$ folded-chance level of the classical GCC-PHAT/SRP-PHAT baselines (Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare}); this demonstrates that the corrected synthetic data carries the azimuth-dependent cues the network learns. The flat $\approx46^\circ$ classical baselines are a narrowband-PHAT artefact (Section~\ref{sec:results}), not a sign that the data lacks bearing information, and the narrowband broadside CRB reference of $0.32^\circ$ (Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare}) is consistent with the signal being highly informative.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Overall Performance Comparison}
\textbf{The headline result is negative for the multi-task hypothesis: joint training does not improve localization accuracy, and dynamic weighting brings no accuracy gain over fixed equal weighting---it only removes the need to tune $\lambda$.} The learned end-to-end representation, not the joint objective or the weighting rule, accounts for the improvement over classical baselines. Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare} reports the ablation variants and the CRB at $\mathrm{SNR}=-5\,\mathrm{dB}$, all reproduced by the accompanying code. The proposed scheme attains Pd=1.00, Pfa=0.00, MAE $7.50^\circ\pm10.38^\circ$, reducing the error of the classical GCC-PHAT and SRP-PHAT baselines ($46.40^\circ$ and $46.16^\circ$) by a factor of about six. A \emph{non-paired} Welch test (unequal variances) confirms that this gap is not an artefact of the independent draws: the mean MAE difference is $38.90^\circ$ for GCC-PHAT (Welch $t=11.12$, $\mathrm{df}=61.5$, $p=2.3\times10^{-16}$, 95\% CI $[31.91^\circ,45.89^\circ]$) and $38.66^\circ$ for SRP-PHAT ($t=11.82$, $\mathrm{df}=61.9$, $p=1.6\times10^{-17}$, 95\% CI $[32.12^\circ,45.20^\circ]$). Because the classical and network samples are not paired sample-by-sample, a paired test is not applicable; the reported intervals are non-paired Welch intervals. The ablation is reported with properly trained single-task references, so that any contribution of joint training can be assessed rather than assumed. The single-task localization reference reaches $5.95^\circ$, which is slightly better than the joint model's $7.50^\circ$, and the single-task detector attains Pd=1.00, Pfa=0.00, identical to the joint detection head. We therefore do not claim that joint training is necessary for, or the primary source of, the accuracy gain. At $\mathrm{SNR}=-5\,\mathrm{dB}$ the dynamic scheme attains $7.50^\circ$ and fixed equal weighting ($\lambda=1.0$) $5.62^\circ$; the latter is slightly better (by 1.88$^\circ$), so dynamic weighting does not improve peak localization accuracy in this setting. Its practical value is that it requires no manual loss-weight search. The spread across the four multi-task variants is 1.88$^\circ$, which is about seven times the Monte Carlo standard error of roughly 0.27$^\circ$, so the fixed--dynamic difference is resolvable, though small in absolute terms.
In the corrected implementation the variance parameters were included in the optimizer and did update (final log-variance $-2.76$ for detection and $+0.94$ for regression, giving precision weights $15.8$ and $0.39$). However, the detection loss had already collapsed to $\sim5\times10^{-3}$ while the regression loss remained $\sim6.3$ (a ratio above $10^3$); Kendall's homoscedastic objective consequently raised the weight of the near-zero-loss detection task and suppressed the regression weight to about one fifth of fixed equal weighting, so the dynamic rule did not improve azimuth accuracy. The CRB of~\eqref{eq:crb} is evaluated for the same 2048-sample observation window as the network input. Strictly, the CRB bounds the variance---and therefore the RMSE---of unbiased estimators; the MAE reported here is a different error statistic, so we treat the CRB as an RMSE-type reference rather than as a bound that the MAE must exceed, and we do not claim the estimator approaches it. It is also a single-angle quantity evaluated at broadside, whereas the MAE is averaged over the test azimuth distribution, so the two are not directly comparable point by point. We note that $\mathrm{CRB}(\theta)\propto1/\sin^2\theta$ diverges as $\theta\to0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$, so a full angular average of the CRB over $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$ is unbounded; we therefore report the conventional broadside reference $\theta=90^\circ$ rather than an average, while the network MAE is the corresponding average over the test azimuth distribution. The error saturation at about $5.4^\circ$ for $\mathrm{SNR}\ge5\,\mathrm{dB}$ is no longer noise-limited but reflects the finite capacity of the regression head together with the 2048-sample observation window and the 4000-sample training set. The remaining gap to this RMSE-type reference ($0.32^\circ$ at $-5\,\mathrm{dB}$, and lower still at higher SNR) shows that the learned estimator does not yet attain the broadside unbiased-estimator benchmark; closing this gap would call for larger model capacity and more training data, which we leave to future work.

The comparison across SNR operating points reinforces this picture. The multi-task variants remain within about 1.9$^\circ$ of one another at SNR$\geq-5$\,dB, and the point-estimate winner changes with the operating point (fixed $\lambda=1.0$ is lowest at $-5$, $0$, $5$ and $10$\,dB, while $\lambda=0.1$ is lowest at $-10$\,dB and $\lambda=1.0$ remains lowest at $15$\,dB), so no single fixed weight is uniformly optimal and the dynamic scheme's advantage is limited to avoiding a weight search. At $-10$\,dB the detection-biased $\lambda=0.1$ setting is competitive ($10.17^\circ$ vs $7.50^\circ$ for dynamic), trading localization for detection margin, though detection is already saturated. In a separate V4 paired protocol (the same $600$-trial test set at both budgets), extending training to $30$ epochs reduces the MAE from $6.80^\circ$ to $4.20^\circ$ for the dynamic scheme and from $4.97^\circ$ to $4.24^\circ$ for fixed $\lambda=1$. These V4 numbers use a controlled paired RNG state and are not directly comparable in absolute value to the V3 figures of Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare}; only the 20-to-30-epoch trend is compared. Under the V3 protocol, identical weights evaluated with a different RNG state can yield $7.741^\circ$ versus $7.499^\circ$, so absolute cross-protocol differences would be misleading. The underlying diagnostic is the drift of the learned log-variances: from $(-2.76,+0.94)$ at epoch $20$ to $(-3.79,+1.27)$ at epoch $30$, i.e.\ precision weights $(15.8,0.39)\to(44,0.28)$. Because $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ collapses toward zero while $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}$ remains at $5$--$6$, Kendall's rule progressively upweights the near-zero-loss detection task; this explains why the dynamic scheme's result depends on the training budget. Both improve, but the dynamic scheme's larger dependence on the training budget---its learned weights keep drifting through epoch $30$ while the fixed weights do not---is a practical engineering disadvantage. We draw no conclusion about relative robustness from these single runs. All reported main results use the $20$-epoch budget.

\textbf{Summary of the overall comparison.} Three observations stand out and are carried through the rest of the paper. First, the roughly sixfold error reduction over classical TDOA comes from the learned end-to-end representation, not from the joint training objective: the properly trained Solo-Loc reference is at least as accurate as the joint model. Second, dynamic weighting brings no accuracy gain over fixed equal weighting; its only benefit is the absence of a manual loss-weight search, and fixed weights are slightly better here. Third, the dynamic rule is sensitive to the training budget because its learned weights keep drifting, so its reported advantage is not stable across budgets. Because detection is saturated at every tested SNR, these simulations isolate localization and do not exercise a genuine detection operating point; we turn to recorded audio next.

\subsection{SNR Sensitivity}
Fig.~\ref{fig:snr} plots $P_d$ and the $180^\circ$-folded azimuth error versus SNR from \texttt{data\_snr.csv}. The detection probability is fully saturated at $P_d=1.00$ across the tested range. The proposed method's folded error lies above the narrowband broadside CRB reference at every SNR; the two are different error statistics (Section~\ref{sec:results}). For reference, the classical GCC-PHAT and SRP-PHAT baselines remain near $45^\circ$ folded azimuth error and are essentially flat across the whole SNR range (Fig.~\ref{fig:snr}). In plain terms, PHAT whitens every frequency bin, so an \SI{800}{\hertz} tone occupying only about $20$ of the $1024$ frequency bins competes with thousands of noise-dominated bins at equal weight. Moreover, the carrier's $20$-sample period is much longer than the largest adjacent-element delay of $3.73$ samples, so the correlation has several nearly equivalent peaks and the picked lag is effectively arbitrary within a period (Section~\ref{sec:related}). This is an intrinsic limitation of PHAT on tonal signals, not a generic noise-level effect. On this six-element ULA, SRP-PHAT reduces to a GCC-PHAT-like steered-response scan, so the two classical curves stay within a few degrees of one another and both stay in the $43^\circ$--$49^\circ$ band over the whole range. This is the gap the learned model closes. Note, however, that the classical baselines have no detector of their own and that the saturated detection task isolates localization only; no detection benefit from joint training is observable here.

\subsection{Real-World Validation on Recorded Drone Audio}
\label{sec:real}
All results above are numerical simulations with the six-element ULA and the raw-waveform frontend of Sections~\ref{sec:signal}--\ref{sec:method}, and---consistent with the limitations noted in the Introduction---they assume an ideal array manifold (no element self-noise, gain mismatch, channel failure, or geometry calibration error). We now test transfer to real signals on two publicly available corpora: the Fraunhofer IDMT dataset, used to assess \emph{network} transfer of the learned model, and the Western Sydney University (WSU) corpus, used to assess the \emph{classical} baselines and, crucially, to obtain a real-world detection ROC that the IDMT set cannot provide (it lacks background-only negatives).

\subsubsection{Fraunhofer IDMT: network transfer to recorded audio}
\label{sec:real_idmt}
\textbf{Limitations to state before reading any number in this subsection.} The IDMT corpus contains drone-present segments only: there are no background-only negatives, so $P_d=1.00$ is meaningless as a detection result (it merely restates that every drone segment is flagged) and a false-alarm rate cannot be computed here. No held-out split is used: the fine-tuned network is trained and evaluated on the same 163 segments, so its folded MAE is an \emph{optimistic in-sample} estimate, not evidence of generalization to new flights. Finally, the folded numbers below are $180^\circ$-axis errors, not full $360^\circ$ bearing errors; full-circle performance is reported separately for the $360^\circ$ retraining experiment later in this subsection. These caveats qualify all folded IDMT results below; background-only detection is supplied separately by the WSU corpus in Section~\ref{sec:real_wsu}.

We first evaluate network transfer using the Fraunhofer IDMT real-world drone audio dataset~\cite{idmt2025drone}, recorded with the eight-element cubic microphone array also used for neural drone localization in~\cite{yang2025eusipco}; it contains 163 thirty-second segments of eight-channel audio at \SI{48}{\kilo\hertz} (PCM-16), and each segment ships with a GPS-derived ground-truth azimuth. The recordings capture several consumer UAVs (DJI Phantom 4, DJI Mavic, and Align models) in outdoor flight, whose acoustic signature is broadband rotor harmonics rather than the single \SI{800}{\hertz} tone of the simulator.

Because the cubic array is neither a ULA nor a six-element array, we use the separately trained eight-microphone network of Section~\ref{sec:geo}, whose geometry-aware GCC-PHAT frontend replaces the raw-waveform input and yields a $28\times 61$ feature map for the eight microphones. Concretely, each eight-channel segment is downsampled to \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz} and framed with a \SI{2048}{}-sample (\SI{128}{\milli\second}) Hann window and \SI{1024}{}-sample (\SI{64}{\milli\second}) hop; for every one of the 28 microphone pairs the GCC-PHAT correlation is computed frame-wise, averaged across frames, truncated to the central 61 lags, and max-normalized to peak 1. We distinguish the \SI{2048}{}-sample \emph{analysis frame} used by the GCC-PHAT frontend from the \SI{1.0}{\second} \emph{evaluation window} used for labeling and supervision: each \SI{30}{\second} segment is grouped into 30 non-overlapping evaluation windows (after downsampling), yielding $163\times30=4890$ windows; the per-window azimuth label is obtained by circularly interpolating the segment's GPS azimuth sequence and folding to $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$, and the fine-tuned model is supervised per window. The exact microphone positions of the IDMT cube are used both to compute the features and as the geometric conditioning input to the heads; the signal is downsampled to \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz} and a single global gain matches the real RMS level to that of the synthetic training input. The simulation's delay model was corrected before all experiments (see Section~\ref{sec:repro}); no results from the defective model are reported. This 28-channel GCC-PHAT network is pretrained from scratch on the corrected simulation and evaluated two ways: \emph{zero-shot} (its simulation-trained weights unchanged) and \emph{fine-tuned} (continued training of the same GCC-PHAT network on all 163 real segments with Adam at a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ for $8$ epochs, using the per-window ground-truth azimuth as target). Because the corpus is small, no held-out split is made: the fine-tuned model is trained and evaluated on the same 163 segments, so the fine-tuned MAE of $18.1^\circ$ is an optimistic in-sample estimate rather than a generalization result, and evaluation on independently recorded flights is left to future work. A cubic array suffers from a front--back mirror ambiguity: a source and its mirror image about the array plane produce identical TDOAs, an inherent physical limitation of the array geometry rather than a deficiency of the algorithm. We therefore fold the azimuth into $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$ and report the error as circular error under the $180^\circ$\, periodicity.
\paragraph{Preprocessing details.}
We state the preprocessing conventions explicitly for reproducibility. The eight IDMT microphone coordinates are expressed in \emph{metres} in a local right-handed frame with $x$ pointing north, $z$ upward and $y$ completing the frame (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}); the measured coordinates, rather than an idealised lattice, are used both to form the features and as the conditioning input to the heads. The 28 GCC channels are the $\binom{8}{2}=28$ unordered microphone pairs enumerated in lexicographic order $(i,j)$ with $i<j$, namely $(0,1),(0,2),\ldots,(0,7),(1,2),\ldots,(6,7)$. For pair $(i,j)$ the PHAT cross-spectrum is $X_i(f)X_j^{*}(f)$ normalised by its magnitude, so the cross-correlation peaks at the lag equal to the delay of channel $j$ relative to channel $i$; after zero-lag centring we retain $61$ lag bins ($-30,\ldots,+30$), spaced by $1/f_s=62.5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ and spanning $\pm1.875\,\mathrm{ms}$. The lag width is chosen from the array aperture and sampling clock: the largest microphone-pair separation in the measured IDMT cube is $d_{\max}\approx\SI{0.225}{\meter}$, giving a maximum physical delay $d_{\max}/c\approx\SI{0.656}{\milli\second}$, i.e.\ $d_{\max}f_s/c\approx10.5$ sampling intervals. The $\pm30$-lag window is a conservative margin of about three times that physical range (the code comment likewise notes a maximum IDMT TDOA of roughly $10$ samples); it is a frontend design choice, not an estimate of the true delay extent. The original \SI{48}{\kilo\hertz} recordings are resampled to the \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz} model rate with a polyphase FIR anti-aliasing filter (rational resampling with an up/down ratio of $1/3$); the target Nyquist frequency is \SI{8}{\kilo\hertz}, so rotor harmonics above it are removed and cannot be recovered. Each one-second window is mean-removed per channel; its $28\times61$ GCC map is then divided by its own maximum absolute value so that the peak is unity, and a single global gain (the inverse median per-window RMS estimated over the corpus) matches the real signal level to the synthetic training scale. These normalisations are applied uniformly to the zero-shot and fine-tuned evaluations.


Table~\ref{tab:real} and Fig.~\ref{fig:real} summarize the outcome. Zero-shot, the azimuth MAE is $45.6^\circ$\, with a correlation of $-0.21$ between prediction and folded ground truth: the simulator uses a simplified \SI{800}{\hertz} amplitude-modulated single tone, whereas real drones radiate rich rotor harmonics and broadband noise, so the simulation-trained regressor sees a different acoustic domain and does not transfer. After fine-tuning on the 163 real segments, however, the azimuth MAE drops to $\mathbf{18.1^\circ}$\, and the correlation rises to $\mathbf{+0.73}$, and the per-type breakdown shows consistent gains (Mavic $12.7^\circ$, Phantom4 $18.3^\circ$, Align $26.7^\circ$). This resolves the zero-shot failure: given real-world supervision, the localization branch adapts to real recordings once the frontend is geometry-aware and the simulation properly encodes time-delay information. The persistent zero-shot gap and the weaker Align-class error point to the broadband acoustic domain gap and to data scarcity as the open problems, not to a flawed architecture: the Align class shows the largest error ($26.7^\circ$) because it contributes the smallest number of recorded segments ($n=16$), and enlarging its recordings would allow a more reliable per-type assessment. As noted above, the fine-tuned figures are in-sample results---the model is evaluated on the same 163 segments used for adaptation---and should be read as an optimistic in-sample estimate, not a generalization result.
\paragraph{Folded-error disclosure.}
The $18.05^\circ$ (fine-tuned) and $45.55^\circ$ (zero-shot) figures of Table~\ref{tab:real} are \emph{folded} errors on the $180^\circ$ azimuth axis (both labels and predictions taken modulo $180^\circ$), not full-circle bearing errors. Accordingly, the folded results characterize only the $180^\circ$ azimuth axis; full-circle capability is established separately by the $360^\circ$ retraining experiment below and is not inferred from the folded numbers. When the segment-level predictions are unfolded to the true $360^\circ$ circle, the real circular error is approximately $78^\circ$--$103^\circ$, depending on the segment-level aggregation convention. The cause is a label-space limitation rather than a tuning failure: of the 163 IDMT segments, 68 have ground-truth azimuth in $(180^\circ,360^\circ]$, whereas every segment-level prediction lies in $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$. Because the training targets were folded modulo $180^\circ$, the network has no output capable of representing the back half of the circle, so those segments are necessarily mapped to their mirror direction and incur a large unfolded error; this is the same front--back mirror ambiguity noted for the WSU corpus in Section~\ref{sec:real_wsu}. Three remedies are conceivable: estimate elevation together with azimuth so that the source half-plane is resolved, use an array geometry without a front--back mirror symmetry, or train with unfurled $360^\circ$ labels and report the full-circle error. We implement the third remedy in a separate v3-architecture re-run (the same batch as the comparators below): training with unfurled $360^\circ$ labels and a $360^\circ$ circular loss reduces the full-circle MAE from $138.4^\circ$ (the folded-label fine-tuned model evaluated on the full circle) to $31.9^\circ$; predictions then span $[84^\circ,267^\circ]$, confirming that the back half of the circle is represented, and the 68 segments with ground truth above $180^\circ$ incur a full-circle error of $37.3^\circ$. The trade-off is explicit: under the folded metric the retrained model degrades from $19.09^\circ$ to $24.4^\circ$ (v3-architecture re-run values), and the full-circle result is still an in-sample estimate on the same 163 segments. To pre-empt a possible cherry-picking concern, we state that the folded MAE of Table~\ref{tab:real} is obtained by computing the circular $180^\circ$ error of each of the 30 one-second windows in a segment and then averaging over windows (the \texttt{ang\_err} column of the released per-segment CSV), not by comparing a single segment-level prediction with a single segment-level ground-truth value. The two aggregation conventions give different numbers, and the window-averaged convention is applied uniformly to all IDMT results reported here.
\textbf{Training-label trade-off: full $360^\circ$ versus folded $180^\circ$.} Table~\ref{tab:label_tradeoff} collects the paired re-run in which the only change is whether the training labels are folded modulo $180^\circ$ or unfurled to the full $360^\circ$ circle. \textbf{The two error scales are reported in separate blocks and are not interchangeable}: the folded error lies in $[0^\circ,90^\circ]$ with chance level $45^\circ$, whereas the full-circle error lies in $[0^\circ,180^\circ]$ with chance level $90^\circ$; a value from one scale must never be subtracted from a value on the other. Every number in the table is \textbf{in-sample} (all 163 segments participate in fine-tuning and evaluation), and the re-run folded value is not a replacement for the V3 main-protocol result of $18.05^\circ$ in Table~\ref{tab:real}.

\paragraph{Ground-truth provenance and data split.}
The IDMT azimuth ground truth is derived from the onboard GPS/attitude CSV supplied with each recording (a sensor stream also available at deployment) and is used only as an evaluation reference and as the fine-tuning supervision target; it never enters the network input, which consists solely of GCC-PHAT features. The in-sample caveat above therefore arises solely because all 163 segments participate in fine-tuning, not from any leakage of ground truth into the input features. \textbf{There is no flight-wise independent test set: these results cannot prove generalization to a completely new flight, and the low in-sample error must not be read as a field-ready accuracy estimate. We recommend evaluating with GroupKFold grouped by flight, so that every held-out fold contains flights unseen during fine-tuning; we implement this in future work.}


\paragraph{Detection note:}
The detection probability is $1.00$ in both settings, but this is of limited significance: the IDMT corpus contains only drone-present segments, so the false-alarm rate is undefined (no negative samples) and $P_d=1.00$ merely restates that every drone segment is flagged. The detection/false-alarm results of Table~\ref{tab:overall_compare} therefore rest entirely on the synthetic study; a meaningful real false-alarm study requires background-only negatives, which we supply via the WSU corpus in Section~\ref{sec:real_wsu}. A complementary, explicitly cross-domain exploration pairs the $4890$ IDMT drone-positive windows with $861$ WSU \texttt{no\_drone} background windows ($5751$ scored windows in total); the arrays differ (eight-element IDMT cube versus 24-element WSU array), so this is an exploratory feasibility test, not a same-array false-alarm validation. Ranking is strong (AUC $=0.998$; Hanley--McNeil 95\% CI $[0.9974, 0.9991]$; frame-block bootstrap with $25$-frame blocks, $1000$ replicates, $[0.9965, 0.9994]$), but this is a \textbf{cross-domain exploratory result: the negatives are WSU ambient recordings, not IDMT-native background}. \textbf{This $AUC=0.998$ must not be reported as an actual detection-performance figure for the counter-UAV task: the positives and negatives come from different arrays (eight-element IDMT cube versus 24-element WSU array) and different recording conditions, so the domain shift itself can produce near-perfect separation. It is a feasibility probe on cross-array transfer only, and a same-array evaluation with native background negatives is required before any detection claim can be made.} Moreover, $63.5\%$ of IDMT positives have a score of exactly $1.0$ while the median WSU negative score is $0.565$, so the near-perfect separation may partly reflect the domain gap and must be confirmed with native negatives before treating it as a detection claim. The default threshold $\gamma=0.5$ is badly mismatched across domains ($P_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.541$). With NP recalibration (Wilson intervals), targeting $P_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.01$ selects $\gamma^*=0.9834$ and yields $P_d=0.9843$ $[0.9804,0.9874]$, whereas targeting $P_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.05$ selects $\gamma^*=0.9428$ and yields $P_d=0.9939$. The lesson is that cross-domain detection requires threshold recalibration: false-alarm behaviour measured at $\gamma=0.5$ in one domain must not be extrapolated to another.

\paragraph{Setup assumptions:}
Two pipeline assumptions warrant stating. First, the eight WAV channels are assumed to map one-to-one to the eight microphones per the IDMT geometry; a channel permutation would rotate the reconstructed array and bias every estimate, so empirical calibration of this mapping is advisable before field deployment. Second, the IDMT ground truth stores elevation as a zenith angle ($90^\circ$ = horizontal plane) and the flights are near-horizontal, so we report azimuth only and leave elevation estimation to future work.

\subsubsection{Western Sydney University corpus: classical baselines and detection}
\label{sec:real_wsu}
\textbf{Scope warning: the learned network is \emph{not} evaluated on WSU data.} \textbf{Every WSU number below is a classical result; none of them validates the proposed learning-based detector or regressor on this corpus.} The array has 24 elements rather than the 6/8 elements of our networks, and we perform no domain adaptation to this geometry; all WSU numbers below concern classical GCC-PHAT/SRP-PHAT localization and classical energy/SRP-power detection only. Because the IDMT corpus lacks background-only negatives, we evaluate the classical GCC-PHAT/SRP-PHAT baselines on the WSU UAV Acoustic Localization Dataset~\cite{wsu2024dataset} to obtain a real-world ROC. WSU records DJI Air~3 flights on a calibrated 24-element 3-D array (four Zoom F6 recorders, \SI{48}{\kHz}, \SI{32}{bit} integer WAV) with GPS-synced azimuth. We use two sessions: \textbf{test1} (\texttt{Oct\_11\_2024/flight\_5}, an unseen quiet site, \num{1592} drone frames) and \textbf{test2} (\texttt{Mar\_18\_2025/flight\_2}, loud wind/traffic, \num{11927} drone frames plus a labeled background-only tail, seconds 1577--1864, \num{2871} frames). Signals are band-limited to \SI{200}{}--\SI{4000}{\Hz} and framed at \SI{0.1}{\s}; GPS rows are aligned to frames by nearest index. Ground-truth azimuth is reproduced in a local ENU frame (the UTM offset cancels), with a per-session north-alignment offset from \texttt{initial\_azimuth\_deg}. For each of the $\binom{24}{2}=276$ pairs we take the PHAT peak lag as the TDOA and fuse by least squares ($\mathbf{M}\mathbf{d}=c\boldsymbol{\tau}$, no robust rejection); SRP-PHAT grid-searches azimuth$\times$elevation over $[0^\circ,360^\circ)\times[0^\circ,60^\circ]$ ($2^\circ\times4^\circ$), taking the peak steered response. Detection uses frame energy and the SRP-power peak, with ROC/AUC from 200 thresholds (drone frames positive, \texttt{no\_drone} negative).

Table~\ref{tab:wsu_mae} reports the azimuth MAE folded to $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$ (the IDMT convention). For a uniform-random estimate the expected folded error is exactly $45^\circ$, so $\approx45^\circ$ means the \emph{chance level} of this metric, not a resolved bearing. On real data GCC-PHAT reaches $44.5^\circ$--$45.4^\circ$ and SRP-PHAT $37.5^\circ$ (test1) to $45.4^\circ$ (test2)---consistent with, not a reproduction of, the flat synthetic baseline of Section~\ref{sec:results} ($\approx45^\circ$ GCC-PHAT and $\approx44.9^\circ$ SRP-PHAT, flat from $-10$ to $+15$ dB SNR).

The flatness is \emph{not} a spatial-aliasing effect. The synthetic rotor signature is an \SI{800}{\hertz} carrier amplitude-modulated at \SI{120}{\hertz} (Section~\ref{sec:signal}), well below the ULA spatial Nyquist $c/(2d)\approx\SI{2.14}{\kHz}$, so no grating lobe is present. The mechanism is narrowband TDOA periodicity: at $f_s=\SI{16}{\kilo\hertz}$ the carrier period is $20$ samples, about five times the largest adjacent-element delay $df_s/c\approx3.73$ samples, so one measured inter-element phase advance is compatible with several integer-period delays. PHAT then normalizes every frequency bin by its magnitude, giving the sidebands and the numerous noise-dominated bins the same weight as the carrier; the cross-correlation therefore has several near-equal peaks and the selected lag is effectively arbitrary within a period. This is an intrinsic defect of the PHAT whitening on a tonal input, not merely the presence of noise bins, and it persists at high SNR, producing the flat $\approx45^\circ$ folded error of Fig.~\ref{fig:snr}. On the real \num{24}-element array the same $\approx45^\circ$ for GCC-PHAT reflects fragile least-squares TDOA fusion without outlier rejection, whereas SRP-PHAT's grid search is more robust---why SRP-PHAT beats GCC-PHAT on real data while the two coincide under the simplified synthetic model.

Folding conceals the front--back ($180^\circ$) ambiguity inherent to a low-elevation source: on test1, $17\%$ (GCC-PHAT) and $38\%$ (SRP-PHAT) of frames are flipped, and the folded-to-unfolded MAE goes from $44.51^\circ$ to $88.98^\circ$ for GCC-PHAT and from $37.46^\circ$ to $105.33^\circ$ for SRP-PHAT; on test2\_drone the corresponding changes are $45.37^\circ\to90.07^\circ$ (GCC-PHAT) and $45.39^\circ\to79.78^\circ$ (SRP-PHAT). Table~\ref{tab:wsu_roc} and Fig.~\ref{fig:wsu} give the detection ROC. Energy retains some discrimination on quiet test1 ($AUC=0.67$) but degrades to $AUC=0.50$ (random) on loud test2; the SRP-power statistic stays near chance ($AUC\le0.53$ everywhere). test1 contributes only \num{54} background-only frames (a short ambient tail within the same \texttt{Oct\_11\_2024/flight\_5} session), so its energy-detector $AUC=0.669$ has a nominal 95\% Hanley--McNeil interval of $[0.605,0.733]$, while the SRP-power statistic on the same subset is $0.528$ $[0.451,0.605]$ and is not significantly above chance; test2's \num{2871} negatives, by contrast, come from a dedicated labeled background-only recording (seconds 1577--1864). On loud test2 and on the combined set both detectors are near chance (energy $0.500$ $[0.488,0.512]$; SRP-power $0.492$ $[0.481,0.504]$ combined). These confidence intervals are nominal only: consecutive frames spaced \SI{0.1}{\s} are strongly autocorrelated and the \num{54} test1 negatives come from a single 5.3-s segment, so the effective sample size is far below the frame count; the $0.67$ value is therefore indicative rather than a robust detection claim. \textbf{The implication is direct: an energy detector at $AUC=0.50$ is statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip, so under loud ambient noise it supplies no usable detection signal at all. Simple energy and spatial-coherence detectors are therefore unreliable in real, noisy environments, which is precisely why a learned detector with a calibrated operating point is valuable.} The present result---classical detection collapses as ambient grows, and $\gamma^*$'s constancy cannot be relied upon off the synthetic set---is the empirical confirmation of that section's warning.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented an end-to-end network for drone detection and $180^\circ$ azimuth-axis estimation from the raw multichannel waveforms of a six-element MEMS microphone array, and examined explicitly whether its joint design and loss-weighting rule are responsible for its accuracy. They are not: with properly trained single-task references, localization reaches $5.95^\circ$ against $7.50^\circ$ for the joint model, and the single-task detector attains Pd=1.00, Pfa=0.00, identical to the joint detection head. Fixed equal weighting reaches $5.62^\circ$, so the dynamic rule of~\eqref{eq:dynamic} offers no accuracy advantage; its value is that it removes the cost of a manual $\lambda$ search. Detection is saturated ($P_d=1.00$, $P_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.00$) throughout, so these simulations do not exercise partial-detection operating points. The learned model nevertheless cuts the azimuth error of the classical GCC-PHAT and SRP-PHAT baselines by about six, so the gain comes from the learned end-to-end mapping rather than from the multi-task combination.

The real-world test of Section~\ref{sec:real} shows that, once a \emph{separate} eight-microphone network with a geometry-aware GCC-PHAT frontend on the true cubic array is pretrained on the corrected simulation and then fine-tuned, the learned detection-and-azimuth pipeline adapts to recorded drone audio (the six-microphone raw-waveform network is not reused and no weights are shared between the two): fine-tuning on the 163 real segments reduces the $180^\circ$-folded azimuth MAE from $45.55^\circ$ to $18.05^\circ$, with a prediction--ground-truth correlation of $0.73$. These are folded errors on the chosen azimuth axis; full-circle bearing accuracy is supplied by the separate $360^\circ$ retraining experiment rather than inferred from them. In the v3-architecture re-run, unfurled-label training improves full-circle MAE from $138.4^\circ$ to $31.9^\circ$ while folded MAE rises from $19.09^\circ$ to $24.4^\circ$, an explicit trade-off between the two metrics; the folded-to-unfolded discrepancy in the folded model is caused by the 68 of 163 segments whose ground truth lies in $(180^\circ,360^\circ]$ while all folded-label predictions lie in $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$. The WSU corpus (Section~\ref{sec:real_wsu}) supplies the background-only negatives the IDMT set lacks and shows that classical GCC-PHAT/SRP-PHAT baselines remain at the folded-azimuth chance level on real field data while classical energy/spatial-coherence detection is near chance under loud ambient noise, directly motivating the learned detector and its Neyman--Pearson operating point. The network itself is \emph{not} evaluated on WSU (different array, no domain adaptation).

The following limitations bound these claims, listed in decreasing order of engineering importance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{No real dataset with background-only negatives on the same array.} No recorded corpus available to us combines drone-present audio with native background-only segments on a single array: the IDMT set has drone-present audio only, so $P_d=1.00$ is uninformative there and real-world false-alarm performance is not established by it. The cross-domain WSU probe of Section~\ref{sec:real_wsu} uses different arrays and is exploratory, not a same-array false-alarm validation.
    \item \emph{In-sample fine-tuning on IDMT without flight-wise generalization.} All 163 IDMT segments are used for both fine-tuning and evaluation; there is no independent flight-wise test split, so generalization to a completely new flight is not demonstrated. GroupKFold grouped by flight is recommended and left to future work.
    \item \emph{Front--back ambiguity.} The reported errors are $180^\circ$-folded; full $360^\circ$ bearing estimation requires unfurled labels, elevation information, or a non-mirror-symmetric array. A companion $360^\circ$ retraining reduces full-circle error to $31.9^\circ$ but trades off folded accuracy ($19.09^\circ\to24.4^\circ$ in the v3-architecture re-run) and remains in-sample.
    \item \emph{Ideal array manifold without hardware measurements.} The simulation assumes perfectly known geometry and healthy channels; no MEMS array built by the authors was measured, so hardware-level validation remains outstanding.
    \item \emph{Simplified source model.} The synthetic rotor signal is an amplitude-modulated single tone, not the rich harmonic spectrum of real drones; the large zero-shot error reflects this domain gap.
    \item \emph{No on-device study.} Inference latency and robustness on embedded MEMS hardware are not measured.
\end{itemize}

Future work proceeds along seven lines:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item extend the model to multiple concurrent drone sources;
    \item add elevation estimation, which the cubic geometry supports and which can help resolve the front--back ambiguity;
    \item develop domain adaptation for new array geometries and recording conditions (including WSU) and evaluate with grouped cross-validation;
    \item model MEMS non-idealities explicitly. Paired, Holm-corrected stress tests show that channel failure, geometry calibration error, and $10\,\mathrm{dB}$ element self-noise significantly increase the azimuth error, whereas constant per-channel gain mismatch does not. The latter reflects the mathematical insensitivity of PHAT whitening and feature-map normalization to a frequency-flat, time-invariant multiplicative gain, and must not be read as learned-network robustness. Frequency-selective or time-varying gain, channel phase mismatch, and ADC saturation are not modeled, so these results cannot be extrapolated to MEMS devices with non-uniform frequency responses;
    \item benchmark other dynamic loss-weighting strategies, such as GradNorm~\cite{chen2018gradnorm}, on this dataset to test whether the counter-intuitive observation of Section~\ref{sec:results} extends beyond uncertainty-based weighting;
    \item build a larger real dataset with background-only negatives and realistic ambient noise so that false-alarm performance can be validated outdoors;
    \item assess embedded deployment, inference speed, and adaptive thresholding under non-stationary noise.
\end{enumerate}
